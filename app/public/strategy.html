Strategy document · Executive Summary

Date: 7 November 2026 Status: Pre-seed / validation Category: Opportunity Operating System (not a job board)

The one-sentence thesis

Job boards answer *"what exists?"*. Karyvo answers *"what should I do next, and why?"* — and then runs the pipeline for you.

The internet solved opportunity discovery in 2008 (Indeed, LinkedIn). It never solved opportunity decisioning. Every platform added more listings; nobody added a decision layer. That is the gap.

What we are building

Karyvo AI is a personal opportunity intelligence system. A user connects one profile (CV + goals + constraints), and Karyvo:

  • Radar — continuously scans the market across 15+ heterogeneous opportunity types (jobs, contracts, fellowships, grants, visas, co-founder matches) and produces a ranked daily feed.
  • Match Engine — scores every opportunity on match, income potential, interview probability, offer probability, and realistic-ness.
  • Career Twin — a persistent model of the person that improves with every application, interview and outcome.
  • Career Score — six proprietary scores (employability, interview readiness, market demand, salary potential, growth, momentum).
  • Dashboard — market, salary and skill intelligence in one screen.
  • Copilot — CV, cover letter, interview coaching, negotiation, learning plans.

The strategic insight: markets 1–4 are all fighting for the *transaction*. Market 6 owns the *decision that precedes every transaction*. The decision layer is small today because nobody built it; it is large tomorrow because it sits upstream of $89B of transactions.

2. Why the decision layer is unclaimed

Four structural reasons nobody has taken it:

  • It's cross-market. A job board can't recommend a fellowship. A freelance platform can't recommend a visa-sponsored role. The decision layer must be *provider-agnostic*, and every incumbent is structurally forbidden from being neutral about its own inventory.
  • It requires personal context. A decision layer needs the person's CV, goals, constraints, risk tolerance, family situation, visa status. Job boards have none of this and have no incentive to ask.
  • It requires honesty. Telling a user "don't apply, you're 15% likely" reduces engagement in the short term. Ad-funded companies cannot afford honesty. Subscription companies can.
  • It was economically impossible pre-LLM. Entity resolution + normalisation + scoring + explanation across 15 sources = a data team. Now = one founder + a few thousand euros of inference.

P1 — The Global AI Contractor ★ PRIMARY

Snapshot: 27–38, based in EU / India / LATAM / Africa / SE Asia, 3–10 years experience, works as (or wants to work as) an AI/ML engineer, data scientist, or full-stack engineer on AI products. Holds 1 job + 1–2 contracts. Found via Mercor / micro1 / Turing / X.

A day in their life: Checks 4 Discord channels and 6 job boards each morning. Copies the same CV into 5 forms weekly. Turned down a $75/hr contract last month because they didn't know the market rate was $110. Has a spreadsheet of applications that is 3 weeks out of date.

Jobs-to-be-done:

  • *"Tell me my market rate today, honestly."*
  • *"Tell me which of these 30 opportunities is actually the best for me — including the contract work, not just the full-time roles."*
  • *"Don't let me miss the one that closes Friday."*
  • *"Tell me what's blocking me from the $120/hr tier."*

Pains (quantified):

  • ~5–8 hrs/week on manual searching and duplicate applications → ~€6–10k/yr of their own time.
  • Rate blindness: typically 20–40% below market on at least one channel.
  • Missed opportunities: high-value contracts close within 72h.
  • No comparison framework: full-time vs contract vs equity is an apples/oranges decision nobody models.

Gains: Higher rate, less admin, better long-term positioning (skill gaps closed before they become blockers).

Buying trigger: A single missed opportunity or a discovered rate gap. This is a "holy shit" moment we can manufacture — "You were offered $75/hr. The market for your exact profile is $95–120/hr."

Objections: "Another subscription." → Answer: show the first rate-gap insight free; charge for the ongoing radar.

Where they hang out: X (AI builder community), Reddit (r/cscareerquestionsEU, r/datascience), Discord (Mercor/micro1/Turing communities, LangChain, Flutter), Hacker News, Indie Hackers.

WTP: €19–29/mo. Will pay annually (€149) if they've won once.

P2 — The Relocating Professional ★ PRIMARY

Snapshot: 26–40, based in India / Brazil / Nigeria / Turkey / Poland / MENA. Wants a role in Germany, Netherlands, Canada, UAE, or UK. Has a degree, often a masters. English-fluent. Family or life-stage pressure to move within 12–18 months.

Decision moment: *"Which of these roles will actually sponsor me, and which of these countries will actually let me stay?"*

Jobs-to-be-done:

  • Filter opportunities to visa-sponsored only — reliably, not by keyword.
  • Understand realistic odds given their nationality, degree, language.
  • Get the full path: role → visa → relocation → family.
  • Not waste months on roles that will reject them at the sponsorship step.

Pains: ~30–60% of applications wasted on non-sponsoring employers; immigration information is fragmented across 20 law-firm blogs and contradictory; €2–5k spent on advisors with variable quality.

Gains: A shortlist of *realistically attainable, sponsoring* opportunities; a step-by-step relocation path; confidence.

Buying trigger: A visa-sponsorship filter that actually works, or a single "you qualify for the EU Blue Card at this salary threshold" insight.

Objections: Price sensitivity (PPP). → Regional pricing tiers (€9/mo India, €19/mo EU). This is essential, not optional.

WTP: €9–25/mo depending on region; high willingness for one-off "relocation readiness" report at €49.

P3 — The Career Pivotter

Snapshot: 28–42, in marketing, ops, consulting, finance, or a non-technical function. Has done some self-study. Wants to move into AI/data/product within 12 months. Fears falling behind.

Decision moment: *"Is this pivot realistic for me, in what timeframe, via which path — and what's the cheapest credible route?"*

JTBD: Skill-gap truth-telling, a sequenced learning plan tied to *real* job requirements (not generic course catalogues), and a realistic timeline.

Pains: Course-hopping with no signal; doesn't know which skills employers actually require vs. which courses are sold to them; imposter syndrome.

WTP: €15–25/mo, high churn risk (they stop when they get the job — mitigate with annual plans and "next level" framing).

Note: This is the highest-volume persona but the lowest-retention. Use for top-of-funnel and SEO, not as the core wedge.

P4 — The Ambitious Early-Career Builder

Snapshot: 20–26, student or <2 years experience. Wants fellowships, grants, accelerators, research, hackathons, first roles.

Decision moment: *"Which opportunity creates the most leverage for the next 5 years?"*

WTP: €8–12/mo (student pricing €5). Low ARPU, but: (a) they become P1/P2 in 2–3 years, (b) universities and programs pay for cohort access, (c) they're the most shareable/viral segment.

Role in strategy: Acquisition engine and brand-building segment, not the revenue engine. Never let it dominate the roadmap.

P5 — The Senior IC / Staff+ Candidate

Snapshot: 35–50, €120k+, considering a move every 3–5 years. Decision is high-stakes and infrequent.

Decision moment: *"Is this the right 4-year bet — company trajectory, equity reality, scope, and long-term positioning?"*

WTP: €40–80/mo, or €500+ for a one-off deep-dive. High ARPU, low volume, hard to reach.

Role in strategy: Year 2+ expansion. Do not build for them in the MVP; their needs (company health analysis, equity modelling, leadership scope) are a separate product.

4. The honest market-sizing caveat

Most pitch decks in this space inflate TAM by using "global recruitment market $200B+." That number is employer spend on hiring — not individual spend on career decisions. Using it is a credibility killer with good investors.

Our defensible claim: €9–11B TAM, €41M SAM, €7.4M SOM by Year 3, with a credible path to €30–100M ARR via institutional expansion. That is a real venture-scale company if — and only if — we own the decision layer.

3. Scenario detail

3.1 Strategic acquisition — AI talent marketplace (20%, most likely real exit)

Why they'd buy:

  • They are in a supply war. Their biggest cost is candidate acquisition and their biggest weakness is candidate loyalty.
  • Karyvo owns the decision moment — precisely the moment a contractor chooses which platform to work on.
  • The outcome dataset (person → opportunity → outcome across platforms) is the single most valuable asset in this category and they structurally cannot build it.
  • Buying us converts a neutral referral partner into a proprietary funnel, overnight.

Why they'd pay €40–120M: at €8–15M ARR growing 100%+ with 88% margins, that's 5–8x ARR — standard for a strategic supply acquisition. The strategic value (candidate funnel + dataset) often justifies more.

What kills this exit: the marketplace fails (Mercor/micro1 valuations compress); or our referral revenue makes us look like a competitor rather than a neutral partner.

3.2 Strategic acquisition — job boards / careers (10%)

Why they'd buy: Indeed and LinkedIn cannot build the neutral decision layer, but they can buy it and run it as a premium candidate-funded tier (exactly as LinkedIn did with Lynda/Learning, Indeed with Glassdoor's salary data). Karyvo = the "intelligence tier" they can't create internally.

Anti-trust/conflict risk: the buyer may have to keep us operationally independent (a "Karyvo, a LinkedIn company" model) — which is fine for us.

Valuation driver: ARR multiple + strategic defensive value.

3.3 HR-tech / data acquisition (8%)

Why they'd buy: our skills graph + comp benchmarks + outcome data is directly monetisable inside ATS and HRIS products (comp planning, skills taxonomy, market pricing). Workday/Personio/HiBob all buy data. This is the highest-multiple path per euro of revenue because it's a data asset, not a consumer app.

Trigger: the Comp-Data API reaching 50+ paying B2B customers.

3.4 Financial services (4%, highest upside)

Why they'd buy: the Opportunity Rail — offer advances, income smoothing for contractors, payroll for global talent. Anyone with a balance sheet and a talent relationship wants the decision moment. Wise, Deel, DailyPay, Earnin, Chime all have this thesis.

Valuation: infrastructure multiples (8–15x revenue, or strategic).

Risk: regulated, capital-intensive. Only credible if the Talent Graph is real.

3.5 PE roll-up (12%)

A €5–15M ARR, 30–40% net margin, 88%-gross-margin SaaS business is a textbook PE asset. At 3–6x ARR this is a €40–90M outcome requiring no heroic growth — the "boring good outcome."

3.6 Stay independent (15% + 2% IPO)

Honestly the most attractive for a solo founder. €10–30M ARR, 35% net margin, 6–10 people, no board. €4–10M/yr of owner earnings. Many founders would take this over a €100M exit with 2x liquidation preference.

3.7 Acqui-hire / soft landing (15%)

If the product doesn't find PMF but the *team and the dataset* are valuable, a €3–12M acqui-hire is realistic. This is not a failure scenario; it's a floor.

3.8 Shutdown (14%)

Base-rate honesty: most seed-stage companies die. This is 14% *conditional on* the Phase 0–2 gates being met. If the gates fail, this rises to 60%+.

3. Top five risks in detail

R1 — No distribution (score 25)

Why it's the top risk: the product can be excellent and still die. Karyvo is entering a category with zero brand awareness, against incumbents with billions of monthly impressions.

Mitigation plan (specific, dated):

  • Weeks 1–2: 25 interviews + waitlist page + 2 LinkedIn posts → target 100 emails.
  • Weeks 3–8: build in public (weekly metrics post), 5 Discords, 20 DMs/day.
  • Weeks 9–16: first 200 SEO pages, newsletter launch, 10 ambassadors.
  • Month 5+: scale SEO to 800 pages; Product Hunt; YouTube.
  • Kill criterion: if by end of Month 2 there are <300 registered users with no paid channel, the product or the message is wrong — stop and re-scope rather than build more features.

R2 — Churn (score 20)

Why: a career product's natural lifecycle ends when the user gets a job. In a category with 8–10% monthly churn, the model never compounds.

Mitigation:

  • Annual-first (35% discount, positioned as "the whole search").
  • Post-win continuation: Career Twin keeps working — salary tracking, next-move radar, skill maintenance. The product is a *career* system, not a *job-search* system.
  • Reactivation engine: on cancel, capture the outcome (moat data) and set a 6-month reminder.
  • Pro+ tier for continuous career management (higher LTV, lower churn).
  • Measure D30 from week 3, not month 6. Retention problems found late are fatal.

R9 — Founder burnout / bus factor (score 20)

Why: a solo founder building a 12-module AI platform, doing SEO, sales, support and partnerships, is a single point of failure for the whole company.

Mitigation: the entire roadmap is deliberately scoped so one person *can* do it. No marketplace, no social, no native app, no enterprise in Y1. Everything is documented. Contractor support at €2k MRR. Build in public creates external accountability. And a hard rule: ship the MVP in 6 weeks, not 6 months. Long solo builds are where solo founders break.

R3 — Scraping dependency (score 16)

Mitigation: source priority order (APIs > partnerships > user submissions > HTML), 8+ sources minimum, source-health monitoring with automatic failover, and a legal source register documenting ToS position for each. The end-state is that partnerships replace scraping entirely.

R7/R17 — Trust and data quality (score 16 each)

Mitigation: these are the two ways a data product dies quietly. Weekly human audit of 200 random records; duplicate rate as a tracked SLA; factuality guards on all generative output; user reporting on every surface; a public "how we score" methodology page. Never ship a number we can't defend.

4. What would actually kill the company

In order of lethality:

  • Building for 9 months without a single paying user. (Most likely death. Not competition.)
  • Retention under 25% at D30 — proves it's a novelty, not a need.
  • A privacy incident — trust is the entire product.
  • Legal action over scraping — an existential distraction for a solo founder.
  • Founder burnout at month 7 with no revenue.
  • A funded competitor giving it away free — only lethal if we haven't accumulated outcome data.

Notice what's *not* on the list: infrastructure costs, LLM pricing, competition from LinkedIn. Those are manageable. The death causes are self-inflicted.

Is this worth building?

Yes — but not as described in the original brief, and not for the reasons most people would give.

The business is worth building because of one narrow, real, monetisable insight:

AI-talent platforms have created a global, transparent, rapidly re-priced market for professional labour — and the individual is the only participant without a terminal.

Every other framing ("Operating System", "Bloomberg of careers", "intelligence layer between talent and employers") is *vision language*. It's fine for a deck. It is dangerous as a build spec, because it justifies building 12 modules, a marketplace, a social layer and an enterprise product before anyone has paid €19.

Worth building if: you can get 100 people to pay €19 in 90 days. Not worth building if: you need to build the whole platform before testing that.

The idea survives the brutal test that kills most "super app" pitches: it has a narrow wedge with acute pain and a measurable euro outcome. Rate blindness. That's the wedge. Everything else is expansion.

Verdict: build it. Ship 15% of it.

What would Y Combinator think?

They would like four things and hate three.

What they'd like:

  • "This is a real, non-obvious problem with a painful, frequent decision." ✔
  • "The founder can build it alone in weeks." ✔
  • "It could be a subscription business with 85%+ margins and no capital requirement." ✔
  • "There's a data flywheel that compounds." ✔ (if you actually instrument outcomes)

  • "How do you get users?" If you say "SEO," they'll ask what your traffic is. If you say "communities," they'll ask how many people you've already signed up. Have 100 real users before the interview.
  • "What stops LinkedIn?" The neutrality answer is strong — but only if you can say it in one sentence. "They sell ads to the employers we'd be judging." That's the sentence.

What is the biggest opportunity?

Rate transparency for global AI/contract talent.

Not "career intelligence." Not "opportunities." The specific, emotional, measurable fact that someone is being underpaid by 30% and doesn't know it.

Why this one:

  • It's information asymmetry — the most durable kind of business.
  • It's quantifiable in euros — "you're leaving €24,000 on the table" is the best marketing sentence in the category.
  • It's shareable — people post their rate gaps. Free distribution.
  • It's defensible — it requires aggregated, outcome-informed data that no single platform has.
  • It's immediate — it can be delivered in a 2-minute onboarding, before any paywall.

The "Opportunity Operating System" is the destination. Rate transparency is the door.

What is the biggest risk?

That nobody cares enough to change their behaviour.

Not competition. Not tech. Not regulation. The real risk is that people say "cool" and then go back to LinkedIn, because changing how you look for work is a habit change, and habit changes are hard.

The second-biggest risk is churn, which is the same risk wearing a different hat: even people who care stop caring once they have a job.

Mitigation: make the value *immediate* (rate gap in 2 minutes, not "over time you'll see value") and make the product *continuous* (career, not job search).

What is the shortest path to product-market fit?
